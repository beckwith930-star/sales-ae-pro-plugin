# Talk Like TED — 9 Principles Framework

*Based on Carmine Gallo's "Talk Like TED: The 9 Public-Speaking Secrets of the World's Top Minds"*

---

## Principle 1 — Unleash the Master Within (Passion & Authenticity)

**What it means**: Great presentations are driven by genuine passion for the topic. Authenticity reads — audiences can tell the difference between a speaker who cares and one who is going through the motions.

**In a sales context**: Does the presenter visibly believe in what they're selling and why it matters? Does the deck reflect a point of view, not just a catalog of facts?

**Red flags**: Generic language, no personal conviction in the content, slides that could be from any vendor.

**Coaching prompt**: "What's the one thing about this that genuinely excites you? That should be on slide 1."

**Scoring guide**: 5 = The presenter's conviction is unmistakable. 1 = The content is detached and transactional.

---

## Principle 2 — Master the Art of Storytelling

**What it means**: Structure the presentation as a story, not a list. Every great story has a protagonist who faces a challenge and is transformed. In B2B sales: the customer is the hero; your solution enables their transformation.

**In a sales context**: Is there a customer story arc? Is the prospect able to see themselves in the narrative?

**Red flags**: Bullet-point-only decks, company history slides, feature lists without context.

**Structure to use**: Setup (their world today) → Conflict (the pain or challenge) → Resolution (the transformed state your solution enables).

**Scoring guide**: 5 = Clear narrative arc, customer as hero. 1 = No narrative — just information delivery.

---

## Principle 3 — Have a Conversation

**What it means**: The best presentations feel like dialogue, not monologue. The speaker invites participation, makes eye contact, uses "you" more than "we," and pauses for responses.

**In a slide context**: Are there natural pause points? Are questions built into the flow? Does the language invite engagement?

**Red flags**: Wall-of-text slides, no questions embedded, presenter-centric language ("We offer…" "Our platform…").

**Coaching prompt**: Rewrite every "We/Our" to "You/Your" where possible.

**Scoring guide**: 5 = Designed for dialogue; feels interactive. 1 = Lecture format; no invitation to engage.

---

## Principle 4 — Teach Me Something New

**What it means**: Audiences remember presentations that gave them a new insight — something they didn't know and couldn't have figured out alone. This is the principle most violated in corporate presentations.

**In a sales context**: Does the deck teach the prospect something about their own business, their industry, or their problem that they'll take away regardless of whether they buy?

**Red flags**: All-product slides, no industry benchmarks or insights, no counterintuitive observations.

**The test**: If you removed every mention of your product, would there still be value in the first 5 slides?

**Scoring guide**: 5 = Audience learns something genuinely new. 1 = Zero novel insight — pure pitch.

---

## Principle 5 — Deliver Jaw-Dropping Moments

**What it means**: Every memorable presentation has one moment designed to stop the audience cold. It can be a striking statistic, a live demo, a bold visual, a short video, or a prop.

**In a sales context**: Is there one slide or moment that creates a "wow" — a number that reframes the scale of the problem, a live demo that shows the solution working, a reference customer result that shifts belief?

**Red flags**: Every slide is equally important (none are memorable). No designed anchor.

**Coaching prompt**: "What is the one thing you want them to remember 48 hours from now? Design one slide around only that."

**Scoring guide**: 5 = Clear, intentional jaw-dropping moment. 1 = No standout moment — uniform, forgettable.

---

## Principle 6 — Lighten Up

**What it means**: Appropriate levity — a relevant anecdote, a light observation, a humanizing moment — increases retention and connection. This doesn't mean comedy; it means the presenter is human.

**In a sales context**: Is there anything in the deck that acknowledges the human side of the problem? A moment where the presenter shows self-awareness?

**Red flags**: Relentless seriousness, no personality, deck reads like a legal document.

**Note**: Score conservatively — levity that falls flat is worse than none. Award 3+ only for levity that works.

**Scoring guide**: 5 = Audience laughs or smiles in a relevant moment. 1 = Zero humanity in the content.

---

## Principle 7 — Stick to the 18-Minute Rule

**What it means**: Brevity forces prioritization. The 18-minute constraint (TED's original limit) forces speakers to include only what matters. In presentations: every slide must earn its place.

**In a sales context**: Does each slide advance the objective? Are there slides that exist because someone wanted them in, not because the audience needs them?

**Slide count benchmarks**:
- 15-min discovery meeting: 5–8 slides max
- 30-min exec briefing: 8–12 slides max
- 60-min QBR: 12–16 slides max (with appendix for backup)

**The test**: For each slide, answer: "If I removed this slide, would the presentation be weaker?" If no, cut it.

**Scoring guide**: 5 = Every slide is necessary. 1 = Significant deck bloat; core message diluted.

---

## Principle 8 — Paint a Mental Picture (Multisensory)

**What it means**: Visuals, video, demos, and physical props activate more of the brain than words alone. Slides that support the narrative visually — not duplicate the verbal content — are more effective.

**In a sales context**: Are visuals used to show outcomes and environments (photos, diagrams, live systems), or just to display bullet points? Is the design spare enough to direct attention?

**Red flags**: Slides with 6+ bullets, no images, tables with 15 rows, charts that take 30 seconds to decode.

**Design principles**: One idea per slide. Visuals support; they don't duplicate the spoken word. High-contrast, clean layouts.

**Scoring guide**: 5 = Visuals create a mental image that words alone couldn't. 1 = All text, no visual support.

---

## Principle 9 — Stay in Your Lane

**What it means**: The most credible presenters speak from genuine expertise and lived experience. They don't overreach into areas they don't own. Authenticity is about boundaries as much as passion.

**In a sales context**: Does the presenter make claims they can actually back up? Are case study results within the AE's domain to present credibly? Does the technical content stay within what the presenter actually knows?

**Red flags**: Overconfident claims without evidence, presenting technical architecture as if an expert when not, customer stories that feel borrowed, not owned.

**Scoring guide**: 5 = Every claim is credible and within the presenter's demonstrated expertise. 1 = Presenter frequently in territory they can't defend.
